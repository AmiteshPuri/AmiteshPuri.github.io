I work at the boundary between applied mathematics and machine learning, on methods that treat the structure of physical systems — their equations, geometry, and dynamics — as something to be preserved rather than discarded.

Academic background

My training is in mathematics. I completed a B.Sc. in Mathematics at Vidyasagar University and an M.Sc. in Applied Mathematics and Computing at the Manipal Academy of Higher Education, working through analysis, algebra, differential equations, numerical methods, optimization, probability, and graph theory. That foundation is the lens I bring to machine learning: I am most interested in the parts of the field that can be stated precisely and reasoned about mathematically.

Research journey

I came to machine learning first through applied work — end-to-end pipelines in natural language processing and computer vision, including fine-tuning generative text models. That work was useful, but it pulled my attention toward the underlying questions: why models behave the way they do, and when they stop being trustworthy.

My MSc research made that concrete. I studied semi-supervised node classification under conditional distribution shift, using distance metrics as auxiliary regularisers and conformal prediction to quantify uncertainty. Reliability under distributional change became a theme I have kept returning to — and it connects directly to my later work on when neural operators fail out of distribution.

From there I moved deliberately into scientific machine learning: neural operators and PDEs, generative modelling of physical fields, and how continuous solution data should be represented for learning at all. My recent projects are self-directed and built around that shift.

Current interests

My work clusters around four connected areas: learning PDE operators and dynamics (scientific ML); flow matching, diffusion, and latent or discrete generative models (generative modelling); dynamical systems, numerical methods, and structural constraints (mathematical structure); and distribution shift, robustness, and uncertainty (reliability). The research page lays out how these fit together.

What I want to investigate

My longer-term interest is in mathematically grounded machine-learning methods for scientific computing: representation-learning and generative approaches that capture the structure of complex dynamical systems while staying reliable as resolution, parameters, and distribution change. I see this as an open problem rather than a solved one, and it is the direction I would like to pursue in a PhD.
